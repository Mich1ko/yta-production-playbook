# Research and script format selection

Three TED-Ed examples provide contrasting published openings and premises. The observations here concern those accessible openings and official lesson descriptions; the suggested full structures are editorial adaptations, not claimed verbatim transcripts or measured retention patterns.

| Research example | Observable approach | Adaptation for this project |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Why you procrastinate even when it feels bad [S2] | Begins with a familiar task and the viewer's impulse to avoid it | Start an emotional explainer with a recognizable moment, then investigate the cause |
| What if every satellite suddenly disappeared [S3] | Introduces one counterfactual and expands its consequences | Define the changed rule, trace effects, then separate the premise from real-world likelihood |
| Can you solve the bridge riddle [S4] | Places the viewer in an urgent fictional predicament | Establish objective and constraints, invite a decision, then reveal a reasoned payoff |

Choose one main script mode and, if useful, one secondary mode. Narration is an audio layer that can support all of them.

| Mode | Narrative design | Common failure to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Narrated explainer | Question, evidence, mechanism, example, implication | Reading disconnected facts over unrelated pictures |
| Second person POV | You face a situation, choose, and experience consequences | Switching between “you” and an unexplained third-person protagonist |
| What if simulation | One changed assumption creates a chain of conditional effects | Presenting invented timelines as scientific predictions |
| Character driven short | Goal, obstacle, costly decision, change, visual callback | Telling the moral before viewers care about the character |
| Mystery or investigation | Evidence, competing explanations, resolution, remaining uncertainty | Withholding the actual answer after promising one |
| Comparison or challenge | Clear criteria, competing options, tradeoffs, conclusion | Ranking options with inconsistent criteria or invented scores |

**Hybrid example:** use second-person narration for a fictional survival story, a what-if premise for the world, and third-person camera coverage for visual clarity. Mark all world rules as fictional. For a factual explainer, choose evidence-led narration and use dramatized POV only as a clearly identified illustration.

Pacing ranges, scoring weights, word counts, and design suggestions in this playbook are production starting points. Test them against the chosen audience, topic, actual voice performance, and channel analytics.
